Remote Sensing: Data Collection, Sensors, and Platforms
Where they agree

Remote sensing acquires Earth surface data without physical contact using sensors mounted on aircraft or satellites (sources 1, 2).

Two energy sources power remote sensing: reflected solar radiation and thermally emitted energy (sources 1, 2).

Sensors are classified into passive (relying on external energy) and active (emitting their own energy) types (sources 1, 2).

Photographic films are limited to visible and near-infrared wavelengths (~0.4–1.0 μm) (sources 1, 2).

Different materials have unique spectral signatures—distinct patterns of reflection, absorption, and emission across wavelengths (sources 1, 2).

Healthy vegetation reflects strongly in the near-infrared band and appears bright or red in false-color imagery (sources 1, 2).

Water absorbs infrared energy and appears dark or black in infrared imagery (sources 1, 2).

Atmospheric effects (scattering, absorption) degrade remote sensing signals and require correction (sources 1, 2).

What each source adds alone
Source 1 — Lecture 2 RS: Definition, Applications and Data Sources

Three distinct data collection methods: Field Survey (on-ground instruments), Office Survey (digitization and data transfer), and Remote Survey (airborne/spaceborne platforms) with procedural steps for each {1:s:1}.

Human vision as the canonical example of remote sensing—the only human sense functioning as true non-contact detection {1:s:2}.

Five detailed photographic film types with wavelength ranges, key characteristics, and common applications: panchromatic (0.4–0.7 μm), B&W infrared, true color, color infrared/false color (with WWII camouflage-detection origin), and multispectral photography {1:s:5}.

False-color band reassignment mechanism (Green→Blue channel, Red→Green channel, NIR→Red channel) enabling discrimination between natural grass and artificial turf {1:s:5}.

Four worked examples with step-by-step procedures: oil spill detection, vegetation health assessment, soil moisture classification, and military camouflage detection {1:s:6}.

Spectral signature table for common objects (vegetation, water, soil, urban surfaces, clouds) across visible, NIR, and thermal bands {1:s:6}.

Source 2 — Lecture 3 RS: Data Sources and Stages of Remote Sensing

Distinction between photographic (analog, film-based, scanned) and non-photographic (digital, electronic, real-time) sensors, with the latter recording directly as digital numbers {2:s:1}.

Three active sensor technologies: Radar (radio waves, air/long distances), Lidar (laser/infrared, high-precision mapping), and Sonar (sound, underwater) {2:s:2}.

Satellite orbits and mechanics: Geostationary (36,000 km, fixed location), Low Earth Orbits (200–1000 km, global coverage over time), and Elliptical orbits with perigee/apogee definitions {2:s:3}.

Three-stage satellite launch sequence with propulsion, directional alignment, and orbital injection using Kepler's laws; avoidance of 2,000+ active satellites in orbit {2:s:3}.

Space debris crisis: ~170 million pieces orbiting; deorbiting protocol using Point Nemo (South Pacific graveyard) where 260+ spacecraft have been safely deposited since 1971 {2:s:3}.

Seven explicit stages of remote sensing: Energy Source/Illumination → Radiation & Atmosphere → Interaction with Target → Sensor Recording → Transmission/Reception/Processing → Interpretation & Analysis → Application {2:s:5}.

Preprocessing algorithms: radiometric correction (digital number to radiance conversion), geometric correction (ground control points and coordinate registration), supervised and unsupervised classification, and NDVI computation with interpretation ranges {2:s:4}.

Constraints table with consequences: solar illumination for passive sensing, clear-sky preference for optical sensors, spectral contrast requirement, atmospheric calibration necessity, and orbital collision-avoidance corridors {2:s:4}.

Trade-offs in satellite sensor design: high spatial resolution (<1 m) typically reduces spectral bands and temporal resolution {2:s:3}.

Applications in agriculture, forestry, urban monitoring, insurance, oceanography, oil/gas exploration, renewable energy, and air quality management {2:s:6}.
