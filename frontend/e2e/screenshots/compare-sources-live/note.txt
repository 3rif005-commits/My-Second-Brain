Remote Sensing: Data Collection, Sensors, and Acquisition Platforms
Where they agree

Remote sensing acquires information about Earth's surface without physical contact by detecting reflected or emitted electromagnetic energy (sources 1, 2)

Two sensor types exist: passive sensors rely on external energy (sun, thermal), while active sensors emit their own energy and detect the return signal (sources 1, 2)

Photographic (film-based) sensors are limited to visible and near-infrared wavelengths (~0.4–1.0 μm) (sources 1, 2)

Multispectral sensors record data across multiple discrete spectral bands to identify features based on spectral signatures (sources 1, 2)

Healthy vegetation reflects strongly in near-infrared and appears bright in infrared imagery; water absorbs infrared and appears dark (sources 1, 2)

Color infrared (false-color) imagery reassigns spectral bands to make vegetation and other features more distinguishable than in natural color (sources 1, 2)

Ground-based methods (field survey, office survey) and remote sensing methods (airborne, spaceborne) are distinct data collection approaches (sources 1, 2)

What each source adds alone
Source 1 — Lecture 2: Definition, Applications and Data Sources for RS

Detailed mechanisms of five photographic film types: panchromatic (0.4–0.7 μm), B&W infrared (up to ~1.0 μm), true color (three primary colors), false color/near-infrared (band reassignment creating pseudo-colors), and multispectral photography (multiple cameras capturing simultaneous bands)

Spectral signature tables showing how specific objects (vegetation, water, soil, urban, clouds) appear across visible, NIR, and thermal bands—a practical reference for interpretation

Color IR false-color interpretation guide with band assignments and resulting colors (red = high NIR = vegetation; dark blue/black = low NIR = built-up/water; green = mixed; brown/orange = soil)

Four worked examples with step-by-step solutions: oil spill detection (anomalous reflectance), vegetation health (NIR thresholds >0.4 healthy vs. <0.2 diseased), soil moisture (NDVI correlation), and camouflage detection (artificial green paint vs. natural vegetation in false color)

Historical context: color infrared film was developed during WWII to detect camouflaged military assets

Human vision as a remote sensing exemplar—explaining why sight is the only human sense functioning as true remote sensing (contact-free detection of distant objects)

Explicitly defined spectral terms: panchromatic, near-infrared (NIR), thermal IR, reflectance, emissivity, false color, digitization

Source 2 — Lecture 3: Data Sources and Stages of Remote Sensing

Distinction between photographic (analog chemical film) and non-photographic (digital electronic) sensors; non-photographic sensors capture thermal infrared and microwave wavelengths inaccessible to film

Three active sensor technologies: radar (radio/EM), LIDAR (laser/infrared), and sonar (acoustic/underwater), with brief descriptions of best environments

Detailed satellite platform taxonomy: two key spaceborne sensor types—Multispectral Scanner (MSS: 4 bands) and Thematic Mapper (TM: 7+ bands with higher radiometric resolution)

Satellite orbits and mechanics: geostationary orbit (GEO, 36,000 km, fixed location, slower temporal resolution), low Earth orbit (LEO, 200–1,000 km, moving ground location, high-resolution mapping), and elliptical orbits (perigee ~200 km, apogee tens of thousands km)

Three-stage satellite launch sequence: Stage 1 (vertical ascent, fuel separation), Stage 2 (eastward direction, secondary separation), Stage 3 (final orbital injection); role of Kepler's laws

Space debris and deorbiting protocol: ~170 million debris pieces in orbit; controlled crash of large satellites to Point Nemo (South Pacific, ~2,500 km from any settlement); 260+ spacecraft deorbited since 1971

Seven-stage remote sensing process (energy source → atmospheric interaction → target interaction → sensor recording → transmission/processing → interpretation → application), with emphasis on where errors accumulate

Preprocessing algorithms: atmospheric correction (radiative transfer reversal), geometric correction (ground control points, polynomial fitting, image registration), radiometric correction, and supervised/unsupervised classification

Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) formula: (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red), with interpretation thresholds (0.7–1.0 = dense vegetation, 0.3–0.7 = moderate, <0.3 = sparse)

Atmospheric phenomena affecting all sensors: scattering, absorption, transmission; constraint that clear-sky conditions are preferred for optical sensors but radar penetrates clouds

Variables and units (transmission T, absorption A, scattering S, reflectance R, emissivity ρ, digital number DN, radiance L, orbital period)

Insight that spatial, spectral, radiometric, and geometric resolutions are interdependent and involve tradeoffs in sensor design
