## K-Means – Algorithm Steps
■ K-Means works iteratively:
1. Choose number of clusters K 2. Initialize centroids (randomly) 3. Assign each point to nearest centroid 4. Recompute centroids (mean of points) 5. Assign points to new clusters 6. Repeat until convergence

## K-Means – Impact of Initial Cluster
■ Initialization matters in K-Means
• Centroids are chosen randomly at the start • Different initial positions → different results
■ Possible issues:
• Convergence to a  local optimum • Poor cluster separation  • Different runs → different clusters
  K-Means is simple, but its result depends on where you  start
17
■ How we fix it in practice :
• Run K-Means multiple times • Keep the best result • Use smarter initialization (k-means++)
■ K-Means++ = smart initialization that improves K-Means results
• It avoids placing centroids too close together • It ensures better coverage of the data space
■ In Python :  KMeans(init='k-means++')

## Hierarchical Clustering – Example
■ Group 6 students based on their math and physics scores
1. Each student is its own cluster.
2. The algorithm then finds the two most similar students and  merges them into a group.
3. It repeats this step: merging the closest clusters step by step.
4. In the end, all students are grouped into one big cluster.
■ This process creates a  tree structure called a dendrogram ,  which shows how clusters were formed at each step.

## PCA Intuition
■ PCA finds directions where data varies the most
• First direction = maximum variance • Second direction = next maximum variance (orthogonal)
■ Think of it as: “Best angle to look at the data”
■ Data points are projected onto a new axis
■ We lose some detail, but keep structure
■ Goal: minimize information loss while reducing dimensions

PCA
■ The first few principal components hold most of the information
■ Principal components are ranked :
PCA1 > PCA2 > PCA3 >...

## Principal Direction (Eigenvector)
■ They define the  best rotation of the data
■ Once we have eigenvectors:
• We project data onto them  • We keep only the most important ones
■ In our example :
• Final PC1 eigenvector: (0.21, 0.98)
o PC1 = 0.21 × StudyHours + 0.98 × ExamScore • Final PC2 eigenvector:(−0.98, 0.21)
o PC2 = -0.98 × StudyHours + 0.21 × ExamScore
Real-world meaning
■ PC1 (keep it):
• The more you study, the better your exam score • This explains 99.7% of all variation in your data. Almost every  student follows this pattern.
■ PC2 (ignore it safely):
• Students who study more than expected given their score, or score  higher than expected given their study time • This is only 0.3% of variation : just  tiny deviations  from the main  pattern.  • These could be:
o Measurement errors
o Unusual cases (sick on exam day, cheating, natural genius)
o Just random noise
